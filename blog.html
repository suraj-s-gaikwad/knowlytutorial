A revision routine you can actually stick to.

Most revision plans fail for the same reason: they are built for a perfect week that never arrives. A routine that works is smaller, kinder and far more consistent.

Start small and protect the time

Thirty focused minutes a day beats a four-hour cram on Sunday. Pick a regular slot, put the phone in another room and decide before you sit down exactly which topic you will work on.

Practise, don’t just re-read

Highlighting feels productive, but retrieval is what builds memory. Close the book and try a question, write out a method from scratch or explain a concept aloud.

Leave room to breathe

Rest is part of the plan, not a reward for finishing it. Build in one lighter day each week, and the routine will still be standing when exams arrive.

Quick takeaways

  • Try past-paper questions on one topic at a time
  • Mark your work and note every mistake
  • Revisit the hardest topics again a few days later

Mathematics 3 min read

Getting the answer is only the beginning.

A correct answer tells you the method worked once. Understanding means knowing why it worked — and when it won’t. After you finish a question, ask yourself three things.

Why does this method work?

Try to explain each step in a sentence. If you can only say “because that’s the rule”, there is something worth revisiting.

What would change if the question changed?

Swap a positive number for a negative, or a line for a curve. Predicting what happens next turns a single answer into a pattern you can reuse.

Can I check it another way?

Substitute your answer back, sketch a quick graph or estimate the size of the result. A second route builds confidence and catches small slips before the exam does.

A right answer is a result. Knowing why it’s right is understanding.

For parents 4 min read

Small habits. More meaningful progress.

Tutoring works best when learning continues gently between sessions. Families don’t need to become teachers — a few small habits make a real difference.

Ask about the learning, not the score

“What did you figure out today?” opens a better conversation than “What marks did you get?”. It shows your child that effort and curiosity matter.

Keep a steady rhythm

A regular time and a quiet place to study help learning become a habit rather than a negotiation.

Stay connected with the tutor

Share what you notice at home — a topic that causes stress or a new interest. Small updates help the tutor meet your child exactly where they are.

Quick takeaways

  • Celebrate effort and small wins
  • Keep the pressure low and the routine steady
  • Talk to the tutor early if something feels off
